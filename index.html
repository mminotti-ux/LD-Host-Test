Reading Logistic Regression

Many business decisions involve two possible outcomes: a subscriber stays or churns, a customer buys or does not buy, an application is approved or denied, or a transaction is fraudulent or legitimate. Logistic regression estimates the probability of one selected outcome.

In the Disney+ example, the model estimates the chance that a particular subscriber will churn in the next 30 days. The result might read: "This subscriber has a 72% probability of churning in the next 30 days."

A probability must remain between 0% and 100%. Logistic regression keeps its estimates within that range. Linear regression does not; when applied to a yes-or-no outcome, it may report a negative probability or a value above 100%.

What Carries Over from Linear Regression?

Logistic regression keeps two familiar questions from linear regression: does each input point toward a higher or lower likelihood of the outcome, and is the pattern statistically significant? The model assigns each input a number called a coefficient. The sign of that number shows direction. A positive sign (+) means that higher input values are associated with a higher likelihood of the outcome. A negative sign (−) means that higher input values are associated with a lower likelihood.

In the Disney+ churn model, the signs point to these relationships:

Disney+ churn model: variable direction
Variable Coefficient direction Interpretation
days_since_last_login Positive (+) More days since last login is associated with higher churn likelihood
weekly_view_hrs Negative (−) More weekly viewing is associated with lower churn likelihood
categories_used Negative (−) Using more content categories is associated with lower churn likelihood
promo_subscriber Positive (+) Promotional subscribers may be more likely to churn

Statistical significance carries over too. As in linear regression, the p-value helps analysts assess the evidence against a null hypothesis of no relationship under the model's assumptions. Analysts consider the coefficient's direction, uncertainty, practical size, and business context together.

What Does Not Carry Over?

A logistic regression coefficient’s sign gives you direction; predicted probabilities give you size.

In linear regression, the coefficient size is often easier to read. If a sales model gives advertising spend a coefficient of 2.50, each additional dollar of advertising is associated with $2.50 more in weekly sales, holding the other inputs constant. The coefficient uses the same units as the predicted sales amount.

Logistic regression converts its inputs into a probability between 0% and 100%. Because the model uses a curved relationship, the same input change can produce a small change in estimated risk for one subscriber and a larger change for another subscriber. A negative coefficient for weekly_view_hrs still means that more viewing is associated with lower churn risk. To describe the size of that relationship, compare predicted probabilities for relevant subscriber profiles or calculate an odds ratio.

Why Businesses Use Logistic Regression

A retention team needs an estimated chance that each subscriber will leave. Logistic regression provides that probability and keeps it between 0% and 100%.

The model can also show which recorded behaviors are associated with higher or lower churn risk. Recent login activity, weekly viewing hours, the number of content categories used, and promotional enrollment may help a retention team decide which subscribers to contact and which offers to test.

Logistic regression can support two business questions:

Logistic regression: analytics type
Analytics type Question How logistic regression helps
Diagnostic Why is churn more likely for some subscribers? Shows which variables are associated with higher or lower churn likelihood
Predictive Which subscribers are likely to churn next? Estimates each subscriber's probability of churn

The model estimates which subscribers may churn and shows which recorded behaviors are associated with the estimate. More complex models may predict well while providing less direct explanation.
